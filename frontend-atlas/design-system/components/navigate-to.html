Components

Navigate to

The destination rail: every place you can reach from where you are standing, written out in words, generated from the same edge list that drew the veins. This page settles what may appear in it, what each entry says, and how it is proved honest.

What this is

You are standing at Office Fig. In a full garden, veins lead out of it to the collection it belongs to, the species it is, the problems it has and the journal you keep about it. Those veins are drawn on the plane, but a vein at fringe scale is a thin line with no name on it, and reading the board is not the only way you should be able to answer where can I go from here. Navigate to answers it in language: one line per vein, each naming the destination and saying in the garden's own words what you would find there.

It is a member of the chrome register, so it takes chrome's material and chrome's voice, and it holds neither a stake nor a chip — inside chrome the two meanings become a leading mark. It is not a menu, not a site map and not a history: it is exactly this node's adjacency, rewritten every time you hop.

The material, the position and the register rules this panel obeys are owned by chrome. The veins it lists and their single source of truth are owned by edge. The journey an entry starts — its one timing, its path along the real veins — is owned by traversal and motion. This page states only what the rail itself decides.

The laws

Law 1 · C1, C3

The rail is generated, never authored. Its entries are exactly RZ.adj[RZ.focus], in that order, and the count in its header is that list's length — so it cannot show a destination the board cannot reach, and it cannot disagree with the drawn veins. “On a focus change the surrounding nodes change to that node's own neighbours, every drawn edge is actually traversable, and the neighbour count tracks real degree.”

With the specimen mounted, run document.querySelectorAll("#rz-navto-body .rz-chrome__item").length and assert it equals RZ.degree(RZ.focus), equals the number in #rz-navto-degree, and equals the number of drawn .rz-vein paths touching the focused card. Hop three times and assert all four still agree.

Law 2 · C14, C15

Every entry travels and nothing in the rail changes data. Each carries the travel mark — data-means="travel" — and there is no second meaning in this panel: a mutation belongs in Actions, and a camera control belongs in the camera cluster.

Assert every .rz-chrome__item inside #rz-navto-body has data-means="travel" and none has data-means="mutate". Activate each one and assert no record field changed. Assert no .rz-stake and no .rz-chip exists anywhere inside .rz-chrome--rail.

Law 3 · C2

The rail is how you get back. Because it is the real adjacency, the node you just came from is in it whenever a vein joins the two — so returning is a hop like any other and never a back button. “After any hop, the origin is still on screen or exactly one visible hop back — with no reliance on a history stack or browser back.”

Note the focus, hop to a neighbour, and assert the previous focus is named by one of the rail's entries. Assert history.length is unchanged by the hop.

Law 4 · C19

Every entry says where it goes and what is there. The destination's own recap sits under its name, so the fourth question — where can I go from here — is answered without a hop, and you never travel to find out whether it was worth travelling.

For every entry assert it contains a <small> whose text equals RZ.nodes[id].recap for that destination, and that the text is non-empty. Read the rail with the plane hidden: each line still names a place and states its condition.

Law 5 · C23

A destination that has not been fetched is listed and labelled, not hidden. Its second line reads “not fetched yet” instead of a recap, and travelling into it still succeeds — an unknown place is a place you may go, and a rail that quietly drops it teaches the map to lie.

Mark a neighbour unknown and redraw: it is still listed, its second line reads “not fetched yet” rather than a fabricated recap, the degree count still includes it, and RZ.go into it succeeds.

The rail, running

The same garden, at the same cells, as everywhere else in this system. The rail on the right is not written into this page: it is drawn by RZ.redrawChrome() from the edge list below. Click an entry, or click a card, or press an arrow key — all three reach the destination the same way and take the same time.

Hop once and watch the header: the count changes because the adjacency changed, not because anyone wrote a new number. Nothing in the rail is a route — the click calls the one hop path, and the camera travels the vein you can see.

RZ.mount({
  world: "#rz-world",
  plane: "#rz-plane",
  focus: "n-office",
  path: ["n-garden", "n-office"],
  edges: [
    ["n-garden", "n-office"],
    ["n-garden", "n-studio"],
    ["n-garden", "n-garden-more"],
    ["n-office", "n-problems"],
    ["n-studio", "n-problems"]
  ]
});

The shell a page provides

A surface hands the rail three things and nothing else: the panel, a header with a title and a place to print the degree, and an empty body. The entries are the runtime's to write. A page that hand-writes an entry has forked the edge list, and Law 1 stops being checkable.

<div class="rz-chrome rz-chrome--rail">
  <p class="rz-chrome__head">
    <span class="rz-chrome__title">Navigate to</span>
    <span id="rz-navto-degree"></span>
  </p>
  <div id="rz-navto-body"></div>
</div>

And this is the shape the runtime writes into it, one per vein — shown so you can recognise it, not so you can author it:

<button type="button" class="rz-chrome__item" data-means="travel">
  Problems
  <small>3 active · all three drawn</small>
</button>

Behaviour

On a hop

The rail is emptied and rewritten from the new adjacency at the end of the hop, in one pass, from the single source of truth — never patched by appending the new neighbours and removing the old, which is how a stale entry survives a rebuild. Its own contents do not animate: the travel you can see is the camera's, and a list that slides while the board moves gives you two things to follow.

Order

Entries are in edge-list order, which is stable across loads, so the rail does not reshuffle under your hand between two visits to the same plant. It is not sorted alphabetically and not sorted by recency — both make the position of an entry a thing you cannot learn.

Length

The rail lists every vein and never truncates, because the board it mirrors has already been thinned: a collection contributes at most three cards, so a real node's degree stays small by construction. If you find a rail long enough to want a cut, the graph is wrong, not the rail — the fix is an aggregate on the board, and it is owned by aggregate. An aggregate appears here like any other destination, because it is one.

When the world is offline

Every entry still works: the last-known world stays navigable and travel is not a mutation. An entry whose destination is stale keeps its own recap and the destination states its staleness in its own words when you arrive. The rail itself says nothing global about the connection — a banner over the whole panel would be a claim about places that are perfectly current. This behaviour is owned by degradation.

Accessibility

  • The rail is not the only way through. Every entry in it corresponds to an arrow direction on the board, computed from where the neighbours actually lie rather than from document order, so a keyboard user follows the graph and not the markup (C18). A direction with no vein is spoken, not swallowed.
  • Real buttons, in order. Each entry is a <button type="button">, so it is reachable by Tab, activated by Enter or Space, and announced as a button. Reading order matches visual order; the rail is never reordered in CSS.
  • The degree is text. “4 veins” is in the header as words, so it is read out with the panel rather than inferred by counting children.
  • Arrival is announced. After a hop the live region names where you came from, what you crossed without opening, and how many veins lead out of the new place — the same number the rail now prints, from the same source.
  • The mark is not carried by colour alone. Travel is an arrow glyph and mutation is a filled square; the two differ in shape first, so the register survives a greyscale screenshot (C14).
  • Direction is logical. The panel is placed with inset-inline-end and its marks with margin-inline-start, so in a right-to-left reading the rail moves to the other side and the arrow leads correctly.

The system-wide rules for focus, contrast and announcement live on accessibility.

Usage guidance

Do

Let the rail be exactly the adjacency. Declare the vein once in the edge list, and the drawn vein, the rail entry, the arrow key and the degree all follow from it — so “Problems” cannot appear here unless you can also see the line that leads there (C1).

Don’t

Add a convenience entry — Settings, Help, the top of the garden — because it is handy. It is a destination with no vein, so the board cannot show the distance being crossed, and the degree in the header stops matching the graph (C3).

Do

Give each entry the destination's own recap: “Problems · 3 active”, “My garden · 12 plants, 1 overdue today”. You should be able to decide not to travel.

Don’t

Put a mutation in the rail — “Water this”, “Archive” — or dress an entry as a stake. Both meanings then live in one panel and the form no longer tells them apart before you click (C14, C15). Every mutation belongs in Actions.

Writing the entries

  • The first line is the destination's name, exactly as its card says it. Never a paraphrase, never “Go to…”.
  • The second line is a condition, not a category: “3 active” tells you something, “Problems collection” tells you what you already read.
  • Say “4 veins”, not “4 links”, “4 edges” or “4 connections” — and never a cell, a row or a slot. The private vocabulary stays private.
  • If a destination has not been fetched, say “not fetched yet”. It is honest, it is short, and it is still an invitation.
