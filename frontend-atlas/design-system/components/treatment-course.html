Treatment course

The ordered course of care a sick plant is put on — what to do, in what order, and by when — checked off step by step inside the card of the thing being treated. This page settles where a course lives, that every step is a mutation and never a way out, and what checking one off is allowed to change: the step, and nothing else.

What this is

A treatment course is what you do about a problem, once the vitals have said the plant is ailing. Spider mites on the Office Fig: isolate it, wipe both sides of every leaf, spray, look again in a fortnight. Four steps, in that order, each with a day it is due — a leaflet from the plant clinic, written in your garden's own terms.

A course is a node in its own right. It has a kind, a cell on the lattice and veins to the problem it treats and the plant it is being run on, so you can travel to a course the way you travel to anything else. It is also the block you read inside that node once you have arrived: it is the third of the four questions the focused card answers on its own — what is this, what state is it in, what can I do here, where can I go from here (C19).

Use a course when the care is a sequence with an end. A single watering is one stake on the action rail, not a one-step course, and a plant's ordinary routine is not a course either — a routine never finishes. If you are unsure whether what you have is a course, ask whether it can be completed. If it cannot, it is not one.

Be honest about where it came from. A course prescribed off a diagnosis we read from your photo carries that diagnosis's confidence, and when we are not sure, the first step is the one that finds out rather than the one that treats hardest. Confidence itself is settled on vitals; a course borrows it and does not restate it.

The laws

Law 1 · C14, C15

Every step is a mutation and nothing else. A step is done with a filled stake; no step contains a chip, a link, or any other way off the card. The course treats the plant you are already looking at, so it never carries a destination.

Query every .rz-course__step and assert it contains no .rz-chip, no <a>, and nothing that calls RZ.go. Press every stake in a course and assert the camera transform, the focused id and RZ.extent() are unchanged afterwards.

Law 2 · C9

Checking a step off changes that step and nothing else. The row keeps its place in the order, no step is removed, reordered or renumbered, and no card moves, resizes or re-ranks — a finished treatment stays on the plant as part of its story.

Record every .rz-n's computed left/top, the id order inside .rz-course, and RZ.extent(). Check every step off, then check two back on. Diff all three recordings: identical, with only data-done changed.

Law 3 · C17, C19

The steps live in the focused card's body. A course you have not travelled to says its progress in one clause of its recap — "Spider mites · 1 of 4 done" — and shows no steps and no stakes at all.

For every .rz-course in the document, assert its offsetParent is null unless its enclosing .rz-n carries data-focus="true". Hop with RZ.go(id) and re-run: at most one course is visible, it belongs to the new focus, and the only element with a scrollbar is that card's .rz-n__body.

Law 4 · C24

Offline, the course stays readable and stays honest. It never hides itself because it could not refresh: it states its own staleness in its own words, and every step's stake is visibly refused or says it is queued.

Call RZ.setState("net", "offline"). Assert the course is still rendered, that its node states when it was last read, that every .rz-stake inside it is refused or announces a queue, and that no node moved. The offline materials themselves are degradation's.

A course is not a collection of sibling nodes, so the density rule does not apply to its steps — a six-step course draws six steps. The rule that does bind here is the one that governs many courses hanging off one plant, and it is owned by aggregate and spacing. Keep a course itself under about six steps anyway: past that it stops being a leaflet and starts being a manual.

Specimens

The world below is live. The mite course is the focus and shows its steps; travel to the fig or the problem — click the card, or press an arrow key with the world focused — and the steps go with the focus while every card stays exactly where it stands. Check a step off first and watch the same thing hold.

The three step states

A step is open, due, or done. Due is carried by the step's own left rule and by its title — never by turning the whole row red — and done recedes without leaving the order.

Behaviour

Checking a step off

The stake flips its own aria-pressed and its step's data-done, and stops there. It is a two-way switch, so a mis-tap costs nothing: press it again and the step is open. It writes no position, touches no other step, and asks nothing of the camera — which is the whole of Law 1 and Law 2 in one gesture, and is why a step is a stake and not a chip.

Finishing

When the last step is checked, the course is finished and stays exactly where it is, fully checked, on the plant. It keeps its cell, its veins and its recap — now "Spider mites · finished" — until a new examination prescribes a new course. Clearing a finished course away is the app forgetting something you did.

Arriving and leaving

Travelling to a course is an ordinary hop on the one timing, along the vein from the plant or the problem — settled once on traversal. On arrival the card expands in place and the steps appear because the card is now shown in full, not because anything mounted. Leaving shrinks it again with its steps still in it.

Under reduced motion, and while it is still loading

A course owns no motion of its own — checking a step is a state swap with nothing to travel — so it reads identically with reduced motion on. A course still being fetched shows a skeleton at its real row heights inside its own card, and the diagnosis a course is waiting on can genuinely take ten or fifteen seconds; both of those are degradation's to state, and both leave the geography untouched.

Attributes and classes
HookValuesMeans
.rz-course — The leaflet. An <ol>, because the order is real. Sits inside .rz-n__body, usually on a .rz-n__section. Never nest one inside another.
.rz-course__step — One step: its mark, then its title, its instruction and its stake. An <li>.

.rz-stake aria-pressed The only control a step may hold. Its refused state, which offline uses, is the stake's own.

Every value above is a token reference, so the palette switcher in Settings re-keys a course live along with everything else — see colors.

Accessibility

  • The order is in the markup. A course is an <ol> and a step is an <li>, so a screen reader announces "list, 4 items" and numbers the steps itself. .rz-course__mark is the same number drawn; if you find yourself giving it a label, you have made it say the number twice.
  • Each stake says which step it belongs to. Four buttons all reading "Mark as done" is four identical accessible names. Add the step's own title in an .rz-sr span, so the name reads "Mark as done — step 2, wipe the leaves".
  • Done is announced by the control itself. The stake carries aria-pressed, and it is the focused element when it flips, so the change is spoken without a live region. Do not also call RZ.say for it — the live region belongs to travel, and a course that announces from both says everything twice.
  • One tab stop per step. Tab walks the stakes down the course in order. Nothing in a course is reachable by hover alone, and nothing in it takes a tab stop that is not a control — which is also why a step holds no link: it would put a traversal in the middle of the keyboard walk of a mutation list.
  • The colour is never the state. A due step says "today" in its title and a done step says "Done" on its stake. Read the course in greyscale and every step still states where it stands.
  • Logical properties throughout. The step's rule is border-inline-start and its columns are laid out inline, so in RTL the mark and the rule move to the reading edge instead of pointing the wrong way.
  • The course does not interrupt the graph walk. Arrow keys with the world focused still follow the veins to the plant and the problem, and that walk is settled on accessibility.

Tab through a focused course and read each stake's accessible name: every one is distinct and names its step. Press each in turn and assert aria-pressed and the step's data-done agree, and that no live-region text was emitted.

Using it

Do

Keep every step something you do to the plant in front of you, with a stake to check it off. The course is a leaflet you work through without ever leaving the plant you are treating (C14).

Don’t

Put a way out inside a step — a chip to the mite guide, a link to the species. The leaflet you were working through takes you somewhere else, and the plant you were treating is behind you. Put the guide on Navigate to, where destinations belong.

Do

Let a checked step turn done where it stands, keeping its number and its place in the order, so you can see what you have already got through (C9).

Don’t

Drop a done step out of the list, float the remaining ones up, or renumber the course. You lose your place mid-treatment, and a course that reorders itself as you work stops being something you can trust with a plant.

Do

Write the due day the way you would say it — "today", "in 7 days" — in the step's own title, and let the left rule agree with it.

Don’t

Print a bare date and leave the user counting, or mark three steps due at once. A course asks one thing of you at a time; if everything is due, nothing is.

Two more rules. Write the steps in the order you would say them out loud, and start a low-confidence course with the step that finds out rather than the step that treats hardest — checking the roots before repotting is both better care and a more honest interface. And leave a finished course on the plant: a treatment you completed is part of that plant's story.
