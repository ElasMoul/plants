Components

Aggregate

A card that stands for the members of a collection the board did not draw one by one. This page owns the density rule — stated once here, for every collection in your garden — and settles that standing for the rest is a place you can go, never a number you are shown and left with.

What this is

Your garden grows. Twelve plants today, forty next spring, and a species list that already runs to twenty-eight before you have bought anything. A board that drew all of them would stop being a board: the veins would cross into a mat, the cards would shrink under their own names, and finding the fig that needs water would take longer than walking to it. So when a collection gets big, Rhizome draws its two highest-ranked members and one more card — the aggregate — which stands for everything else and says how much that is.

An aggregate is a card like any other card. It has its own cell on the lattice, its own veins, its own count in its own words, and you travel to it exactly as you travel anywhere else. That is the whole difference between a design decision and a truncation: +10 more plants is a door, and “12 plants (showing 2)” is an apology.

This page and spacing are the only two places the density rule is written. Every other page that needs it cites it and links here — including edge, which decides nothing about which members get a vein, and world, which draws whatever survives the rule. Your garden's plants are an example of the rule; so are your species, your problems and your journal. None of them is a special case.

The laws

Law 1 · C8

Two and an aggregate. For any node joined to a collection of sibling nodes of one kind: below the threshold, every member is drawn as its own card; at the threshold or above, the two highest-ranked members are drawn plus exactly one aggregate card standing for all the rest, labelled “+N more”, which opens the full list. The threshold is 4 and the drawn count is 2, and both are read from constitution.json → parameters.density — never typed into a component. “Inserting a node leaves existing nodes put, and the design states its placement rule, its density strategy, and its growth ceiling.”

For every collection card on the board assert drawn === (members < RZ.PARAMS.density.threshold ? members : RZ.PARAMS.density.shown), and that an aggregate card exists exactly when members >= threshold, with the number in its label equal to members - shown. Then call RZ.verifyParameters() and assert it returns [], so the number the script used and the number the stylesheet holds are the same number.

Law 2 · C1, C3

The aggregate is a first-class node. It takes a cell from the same formula every other card takes one from, it is joined to its collection by a real vein in the edge list, it is announced and counted like any neighbour, and travelling to it opens the full list. “Every navigation follows an edge that was visible before the click.”

Assert the aggregate's id appears in RZ.EDGES, that RZ.adj[collection] counts it, that it has a drawn .rz-vein at first paint, and that RZ.go(aggregateId) lands on it with the focused card showing the members it stands for. An aggregate that is not in the edge list is a label, and this law is what stops one being shipped.

Law 3 · C7, C8

Rank comes from your garden, never from the alphabet and never from the order records happened to be written. In order: most overdue actions first, then anything needing treatment, then what you open most — ties broken by record id so a tie does not reshuffle. The two cards that earn a place are the two you were most likely to be coming for. “Two consecutive loads of the same graph lay out identically, with positions derived deterministically and persisted.”

Load the board twice and diff the ids of the drawn members of every collection: identical, in the same cells. Then rename the top-ranked plant to something starting with Z and reload — it is still drawn, because the ordering never consulted its name. Give a second plant a longer overdue period and reload: it takes the place, and nothing else on the board moved.

Law 4 · C8, C9

The rule is the growth ceiling. Any one collection contributes at most three cards to the board whether it holds four members or forty thousand, so the picture a user learns in their first week is the picture they still have in their tenth year. Growth changes the count inside the aggregate; it does not change the shape of the board. “No non-structural data change (status, count, new attachment) moves anything.”

Multiply a collection's membership by a thousand in the data and reload: the number of cards drawn for it is unchanged, the aggregate's count is the only thing that differs, and every other card's computed left/top is identical to before.

Law 5 · C23

An aggregate is not an unknown region. It has a solid rim and a count it will stand behind, because we have the data and chose how much of it to draw. A region we have not fetched is dashed and says so, because we do not know what is there. The two must never wear each other's material. “On a partial graph, unloaded regions are drawn as explicitly unknown — distinguishable from ‘loaded and empty’ — with traversal into them allowed and never dead-ending.”

Put an aggregate card and an unknown card side by side and read them with the text masked: the rims differ. Then read them with the text: one states a number, the other states that it has no number yet. Assert no element carries both data-aggregate="true" and data-state="unknown".

Law 6 · C8

Nothing the count covers is out of reach. Every list in PlantPal is paged, so “+10 more opens the full list” is something the interface can actually do — and a count the user cannot get behind is a defect, not a trade-off.

Travel to every aggregate on the board and assert each one reaches its members, page by page, to the last of them. Sum what each aggregate reaches with the members drawn beside it and assert the total equals the collection's real membership.

Specimen

Both branches of the rule, side by side, on one board — because one branch alone reads as an example rather than a rule. Your garden holds twelve plants, which is at the threshold: the two that need you first are drawn, and +10 more plants stands for the rest. Problems holds three, which is below it: all three are drawn, and there is no aggregate anywhere near them.

Click +10 more plants and watch what an aggregate is. The camera travels a real vein to it, it opens in full like any card, and the rest of your garden is inside it, ranked the same way it was ranked outside. Nothing was hidden; some of it was simply further to walk.

The aggregate is in the edge list beside the two drawn plants, which is the whole of Law 2. Nothing about it is special-cased in the runtime: it is placed by the same formula, ranked by the same distance and travelled by the same call.

And the rule itself, read back from the parameters rather than retyped — this is the shape any consumer of a collection should use:

var D = RZ.PARAMS.density;              /* { threshold: 4, shown: 2, … } */

function drawnFor(members) {
  return members.length < D.threshold
    ? members                            /* below: draw them all        */
    : members.slice(0, D.shown);         /* at or above: two, ranked    */
}
function aggregateFor(members) {
  return members.length < D.threshold
    ? null
    : { count: members.length - D.shown,
        label: "+" + (members.length - D.shown) + " more" };
}

RZ.verifyParameters();                   /* [] — CSS and script agree   */

Behaviour

At rest

An aggregate sits in a cell of its own, at the same rank as the members drawn beside it, with a solid rim slightly brighter than an ordinary card's and a quieter fill. It shows its count and says what the count is of, in your garden's words — “10 plants”, never “10 records”, and never a row, a slot or a coordinate.

When you travel to it

It expands in place like any other card and its body carries the members it stands for, in the same ranking they were sorted by outside. The card you came from stays on the board, shrinking where it stands. Long lists page; the last member is reachable.

When the collection changes

Adding a plant changes the number inside the aggregate and nothing else. Adding a plant that is more overdue than either drawn member swaps it into the board and pushes the displaced one into the aggregate's count — a change of which cards are drawn, at the cells that were already theirs, so the shape of the board is untouched. Falling below the threshold — deleting down to three plants — removes the aggregate entirely and draws all three.

When the data is late or partial

An aggregate whose own list has not arrived wears the skeleton every other card wears, at the row heights its members will occupy, and it keeps its cell and its rank while it waits. It never becomes dashed: dashed means we do not know what is there, and an aggregate always knows. The states themselves belong to degradation.

In arrange mode

An aggregate can be dragged like any card, and the offset is held against its own record. It is not a rendering of the collection: it is a node with a place of its own, and it keeps that place.

What a theme fills

A theme chooses how an aggregate reads. It does not choose when one appears, how many members it covers, or whether its list is reachable — those are parameters, and changing one takes an owner ruling.

Accessibility

An aggregate is a neighbour like any other, so it is in the arrow-key graph, in Navigate to, and in the degree the focused card announces. Nothing about it is reachable only by pointer: if you can see it, you can key to it.

Its name is the sentence a screen reader needs — “+10 more plants” — and its recap says what those ten are and what state they are in, so the count is never a bare number floating without a noun. On arrival, the announcement names where you came from and how many veins lead out of here, from the same adjacency the picture is drawn from.

Colour is never the only thing separating an aggregate from a region we have not fetched. The rim is solid against dashed, the count is present against absent, and both cards say in plain words which they are — so a reader who cannot separate two strokes still gets the fact. If we are unsure how much is behind a card, we say we are unsure; we do not print a number we cannot stand behind.

The card uses logical properties throughout, so its rim, its graduation and its list all sit correctly in RTL. Its count is text, not a graphic, and it scales with the reader's own type size.

Usage guidance

Every way to get this wrong is a way of turning a door back into a number. Keep the count honest, keep it reachable, and apply the rule to collections you find boring as faithfully as to the ones you are showing off.

Do

Draw the two highest-ranked members and one aggregate card, joined to the collection by a real vein, and let travelling to it open the full list (Law 1, Law 2).

Don’t

Print “and 10 more” as a line of text inside the collection's card. It is not a place, it cannot be keyed to, it has no vein and it counts toward no degree — so the board's numbers and its picture start disagreeing on day one (C3).

Do

Rank by what the garden knows: most overdue, then needing treatment, then most opened, ties broken by record id. The two cards that survive are the two the user was probably coming for (Law 3).

Don’t

Sort alphabetically or by insertion because it is easier to implement. The board then shows Aloe and Balcony Thyme while the fig that has been dry for two days sits inside a count, and the user learns that the board is not about them (Law 3).

Do

Read the 4 and the 2 from the parameters at the point of use, so a single owner ruling re-tunes every collection in the app at once (Law 1).

Don’t

Decide that species deserve three drawn members because there are a lot of them. One exception is all it takes: the next round reads the board, cannot find the rule, and invents a third. Species is an example of the rule, never a special case.

Do

Give an aggregate whose list has not arrived the same skeleton every other card gets, at the row heights its members will take, and leave it in its cell (Law 5).

Don’t

Dash its rim while it waits. Dashed means we do not know what is on the other side; an aggregate always knows, and borrowing that material makes the one card a user must be able to trust the count of look like the one card that has no count (C23).

Where else this appears

The lattice this card takes a cell from, and the placement formula it shares with every other card, are spacing — which carries the density rule's geometry beside it. The card contract it fills is node; the vein joining it to its collection is edge; the plane both are drawn on is world. It is listed in words by navigate to and in miniature by minimap. Its kind colour is colors, and the rank widths it steps through are typography. The skeleton it wears while its list is late, and the dashed material it must never borrow, are degradation. Dragging it is arrange mode; the wording of “+10 more plants” is voice and copy; and changing the 4 or the 2 is extending this system.
