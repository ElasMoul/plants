Components

Chrome

Chrome is the furniture around your garden: the things that tell you where you are and what you can do, and that must never be mistaken for the garden itself. This page settles what chrome is made of, who its members are, and how the difference is kept material rather than remembered.

What this is

Your garden stands on the plane. Everything else — the bar across the top that says where you are, the list of places you can go next, the things you can do to the plant you are looking at, the small map in the corner, the camera buttons — is apparatus. It belongs to the frame, not to the garden. You cannot travel to it, it holds still while the world moves under it, and nothing in it is a thing you keep.

Chrome is a register, not a layer. A layer is a place in the stacking order, and a place in the stacking order is not something a reader can see. A register is a material: one fill, one corner, one rule, one type voice, one ink strength, one tracking, one way of sitting against an edge. It is declared once as the --rz-chrome-* token family and inherited through the single .rz-chrome class, so it cannot drift one panel at a time. Every chrome member on this page — top bar, Navigate to, Actions, world view, camera cluster, gesture line, probe, live region — is that one class plus one modifier saying which edge it owns.

The difference is drawn inside PlantPal's own palette, type and density. Chrome is not a second aesthetic bolted on; it is the same key, read quieter, squarer and flatter, so it recedes without ever looking like it came from somewhere else.

This page owns the chrome register (L1). The members have their own pages: navigate to, action rail and minimap. What sits inside the frame is world and node. The no-shadow value and the stacking rungs belong to elevation; the two-form grammar of stakes and chips belongs to iconography and interaction and cursors.

The laws

Law 1 · C16, C17

Chrome is one material, declared once in the token layer and inherited through one class. A panel does not choose its own fill, corner, shadow, face or tracking; it chooses an edge, and the register does the rest.

For every .rz-chrome element read the computed style: box-shadow is none, border-radius equals --rz-chrome-corner, font-family equals --rz-chrome-face, text-transform is uppercase, letter-spacing equals --rz-chrome-track, and every member reports the same single font-size. Then grep the page: no rule outside assets/system.css sets any of those on a chrome member.

Law 2 · C16

Chrome sits flush to a viewport edge and bleeds off it, is a sibling of the plane, and never moves with the camera. A node floats on the plane and does; chrome does not. Two visible members never share an edge position: the conditional members — the gesture line, which is shown until you have panned or zoomed once, and the probe, which ships only in review builds — each stand in for a permanent member rather than stacking on one.

Pan and zoom, then diff every member's bounding box: unchanged to the pixel, while every card's box moved by the same delta. Assert no .rz-chrome element is a descendant of .rz-plane, that each visible member's box touches a frame edge, and that no two visible members' boxes intersect.

Law 3 · C14

Neither of the two forms may appear in chrome. Inside chrome the same two meanings become a leading mark on an otherwise typographic row: a filled square means it changes data, an arrow means it travels. The camera cluster is the third category and is the only other control form chrome admits.

Assert no .rz-stake and no .rz-chip is a descendant of .rz-chrome; that every .rz-chrome__item carries data-means="mutate" or data-means="travel"; and that the only remaining control inside chrome is .rz-cam-btn. Click every camera button and assert RZ.focus and every record field are unchanged.

Law 4 · C5

You-are-here is the top bar's centre column, not a second element above it. There is one of it, it lives in the register, and its wording is kept in lockstep with the world view's marker — “Where the user is, is answerable at every zoom level without hovering or reading — by a position marker in the persistent world view, not by a breadcrumb alone.”

Assert exactly one .rz-chrome__here exists and that it is a child of .rz-chrome--top, not a sibling panel. Hop, then compare the last name in the trail with the focused node's name and with the world view's aria-label: all three changed together. Zoom to both ends of the clamp and assert the marker is still inside the world view's frame.

Law 5 · C17

Chrome does not scroll and does not grow. “Nothing other than the focused node's body scrolls.” A member that has more to say than fits its edge is holding content, and content belongs on the plane.

For every .rz-chrome element assert scrollHeight equals clientHeight and that no scrollbar is rendered. Fill Navigate to with a node of degree twelve: the list is still the register's rows and the panel has not become a scrolling column.

The register, assembled

Chrome cannot be reviewed on its own — its whole job is to be un-mistakable for something else, and that is only visible with the something else present. So the specimen is the surface: cards on the plane, and the register around them. Click a card to travel; drag the bare board to pan and watch the furniture stay exactly where it is.

Blur the specimen until no word is legible. The cards are the only bright, soft, floating rectangles on it; everything else has gone to a flat dark band welded to an edge. That is the acceptance test for the register, and it is the one worth running first, because it is the test a user runs involuntarily every time the screen enters their peripheral vision.

Nothing above re-implements anything. The trail in the top bar, the Navigate-to rows and their degree, and the Actions rows are all generated by the runtime from one edge list, which is why an entry can never offer a hop the world cannot draw:

RZ.mount({
  world: "#rz-world",
  plane: "#rz-plane",
  focus: "n-office",
  path: ["n-garden", "n-office"],
  edges: [
    ["n-garden", "n-office"],
    ["n-garden", "n-studio"],
    ["n-office", "n-problems"],
    ["n-studio", "n-problems"]
  ],
  actions: {
    "n-office": ["Log a watering", "Move to another room", "Archive this plant"]
  }
});

Node against chrome

This is the table the system owns. Every row is a channel, and every channel points the same way, so the separation survives losing any one of them — a palette with less contrast, a theme with a flatter halo, a reader who cannot see colour at all.

The two registers (Law 1)
ChannelNode — the gardenChrome — the apparatus
Fill --rz-sheet: lifted off the ground, the lightest surface on screen. --rz-chrome-fill: below the ground, darker than the plane it sits against.
Corner --rz-corner-card: rounded. A card is an object. --rz-chrome-corner: square. Furniture is cut to the edge it is fixed to.
Border --rz-membrane on all four sides: a hairline membrane holding the card together. --rz-chrome-rule on the one side facing the world, weighted --rz-strong in --rz-chrome-key. Chrome has a seam, not an outline.
Shadow --rz-halo-quiet, and --rz-halo-lit at focus: a card floats. --rz-chrome-lift, which is --rz-lift-0. No shadow at all, at any rung, ever. See elevation.
Type voice The body and name faces, mixed case, across the whole prominence ladder. --rz-chrome-face, monospace, uppercase, and one rung only — --rz-chrome-size. Chrome has no hierarchy because chrome has no argument to make.
Ink strength --rz-ink down to --rz-ink-muted: the strongest ink on screen is a plant's name. --rz-chrome-ink, lifting to --rz-chrome-ink-lit only for a row you can act on. Always below a card's strength.
Tracking --rz-track-name and --rz-track-label: set for reading. --rz-chrome-track: wider than any node label, so chrome reads as signage even out of focus.
Position On the plane, at a cell. It moves with the camera and can be travelled to. Fixed, flush to a viewport edge, bleeding off it. It never moves with the camera and can never be travelled to.
Controls permitted Both forms: the filled stake and the outlined chip, plus ordinary links in prose. Neither form. Only .rz-chrome__item rows with a leading mark, and .rz-cam-btn in the camera cluster.

Behaviour

Rows, not buttons

A chrome row is typographic. It fills its panel's width, it is set in the register's own voice, and the only thing that distinguishes what it will do is the mark that leads it: a filled square for a change to your garden, an arrow for a journey. On hover it takes --rz-chrome-hover and nothing else. A row you cannot use right now says so by going faint and refusing the pointer, in place — it does not disappear, because a row that vanishes teaches you that the furniture is not stable.

<button type="button" class="rz-chrome__item" data-means="travel">
  Studio Fig<small>Under treatment · root rot, step 2 of 4</small>
</button>

<button type="button" class="rz-chrome__item" data-means="mutate">Log a watering</button>
<button type="button" class="rz-chrome__item" data-means="mutate" data-refused="true">
  Archive this plant
</button>

The <small> is the one place chrome drops its own voice: a row's second line is the node's own recap, in the body face and mixed case, because it is quoting your garden rather than labelling the apparatus.

Pointer and camera

Chrome is above the plane on the z-scale, so a press that lands on chrome is never a press on the board: the pan gesture cannot begin under a panel, and no chrome member carries a grab or a move cursor. The camera cluster is the only place in the system where a control changes the view without changing anything else — recentre says so out loud, because moving the camera back is the action most easily mistaken for a hop. The full cursor table is on interaction and cursors.

On a hop

The register is redrawn from the graph on every focus change, in one pass: the trail, the Navigate-to rows, the degree beside them, the Actions scope and the world view all come from the same edge list and the same focus, so they cannot tell different stories. Chrome does not animate during travel — it is the fixed thing the motion is measured against, and furniture that slides while the camera slides makes both unreadable. The timing and path of the hop itself belong to traversal.

In the other modes

In arrange mode the world view's button is the way in and the way out, and the rest of the register goes quiet while the cards are being placed — see arrange mode. When the app shrinks into a floating card, its chrome shrinks with it as part of the picture, and the bar that appears beside the card is itself chrome, in the same register, at the same depth — see settings exit. Under degradation the register never becomes a banner across the world: states are material on the cards that hold them, and the probe is the reviewer's way to walk them, not the user's way to be told about them — see degradation.

Accessibility

Chrome is where a screen-reader user is told what a sighted user can see at a glance, so it carries more of the accessibility load than any other part of the system. Every panel is a landmark with its own accessible name matching its visible title — “Navigate to”, “Actions”, “World view” — so the register can be walked directly rather than hunted through. The visible uppercase is text-transform, never uppercase characters in the markup, so nothing is spelled out letter by letter.

The live region is a chrome member and sits on its own rung. It is polite, it holds one sentence at a time, and under reduced motion it holds it longer, because a reader who has turned motion off has also lost the movement that would otherwise have carried the news. After a hop it names where you came from, what you crossed without opening, and how many veins lead out from here. The world view's accessible label is rewritten on the same hop, in lockstep with the visible you-are-here, so the two are never out of step.

The you-are-here trail is a row of real buttons, each one a hop back to a place you actually stood, and the current one carries aria-current. A camera button is labelled by what it does to the view — “zoom out”, “fit the whole world”, “recentre on where you are” — never by a symbol name, and none of them announces a move, because none of them moves you. A refused row keeps its place in the tab order and states why it is refused rather than going silent.

Chrome speaks the garden, never the machinery: a row says “Studio Fig · under treatment”, never a cell, a row, a column or a lattice coordinate. Colour is never the only signal — the mutate mark is a filled square and the travel mark is an arrow, so the two meanings are separable in monochrome. Every member is positioned with logical properties, so the rail, the seam and the marks all reverse correctly in RTL.

Usage guidance

Almost every way this law gets broken looks like a small kindness at the time: one panel that deserved a bit more presence, one action worth promoting, one message important enough to float. Each of them costs the same thing — the user's ability to tell, without looking, what is their garden and what is the app.

Do

Add a new panel as .rz-chrome plus one modifier naming the edge it owns, and let it inherit fill, corner, seam, voice, ink and tracking untouched. If it needs a channel the family does not have, that is an amendment with a recorded reason, not a local override (Law 1).

Don’t

Give one panel a lighter fill, a rounded corner or a soft shadow to make it feel important. The blur test fails immediately: there are now two kinds of bright floating rectangle on screen and only one of them is your garden (Law 1, C16).

Do

Put a mutation in Actions as a row with a mutate mark, and a journey in Navigate to as a row with an arrow. Two meanings, two marks, one voice — and the form still tells you which is which before you press it (Law 3, C14).

Don’t

Drop a filled stake or an outlined chip into a panel because the action matters. The two forms belong to content; repeating them in the furniture makes the furniture look like a card, and makes the card's own stake look ordinary (Law 3, C14).

Do

Say a thing once, in the live region, and let it go. If it needs to persist, it is a state of a node, and it belongs in that node, on the plane, where the user can travel to it (Law 5).

Don’t

Grow a panel into a scrolling column of notices across the world. A strip that covers the garden to talk about the garden is a lie about every part of it that is fine, and nothing but the focused card's body scrolls (Law 5, C17).

Where else this appears

The frame chrome sits against is world, and what it sits against is node. Its members have their own pages: navigate to, action rail, minimap, and the focused card's own answers in focus panel. The no-shadow value and the stacking rungs are owned by elevation; the two-form grammar by iconography and interaction and cursors; the register's wording by voice and copy and its announcements by accessibility. Chrome during a hop is traversal and arrivals; in the other modes, arrange mode, settings exit and degradation. Adding to the family is extending this system.
