Day zero

The garden before there is a garden. This page settles what a brand-new gardener sees on the plane, where their first plant lands, and why that cell is the one it keeps for the life of the record.

What this is

Somebody has just installed PlantPal. They own no plants yet, they have written nothing, and nothing has been identified. What they get is not a welcome screen and not a tour: it is their world, on the same plane as everyone else's, with room in it and two or three things already true — the care guides ship with the app, the journal exists and is bare, and the garden itself is a card with a real count of zero.

Then they add a plant. It lands in a cell chosen by the insertion rule, and that is the last time anything decides where it goes. Twelve plants later, a species arriving, a problem opening, a photo attaching, a slow afternoon: none of them move it. Day zero is a screen most gardeners see once, and it is the moment their whole spatial memory of this app gets founded — which is why it is worth the care.

This page owns first run and first placement. The source it is written from is layout-determinism.json — its insertion_rule.day_zero and its free-cell search are the record; where this page and that file could differ, the file wins.

What this page does not own: the materials that empty and unknown wear, and the difference between them, belong to degradation; the placement formula and the lattice geometry to spacing; the bound on how many members of a collection are ever drawn to aggregate; the 300ms a hop takes, from any trigger, to traversal. Day zero adds no exception to any of them — a world of three cards is governed exactly as a world of thirty.

The laws

Law 1 · C4, C2

First run happens inside the world, never on a surface laid over it or routed to. There is no onboarding screen, no wizard, no tour, no dialog: “Nothing is replaced, unmounted, or routed to — every navigation is a rescale.” Anything a new gardener needs to be told is told in the card it applies to, and the first hop they take is an ordinary hop.

Load the day-zero board and complete the first add. Assert document.querySelector("[role=dialog]") is null, that location.href and history.length never change, and that the element set on .rz-plane before and after the first hop contains the identical objects.

Law 2 · C26, C7

The first plant lands in a place it will keep: “Day zero reads as a world with room in it, with the first node landing in a place it will keep.” Its cell is written onto the record the moment it is added, by the free-cell search, and read back on every load. Nothing later re-derives it, re-balances around it, or renumbers it.

Add the first plant, note its data-cell, then add twelve more and reload. That cell is unchanged, and so is the computed left/top of every card that was already on the board. Load the day-zero board twice and diff the computed positions: the diff is empty.

Law 3 · C26, C8

The room is drawn, not implied. A garden with one card in it is still laid out on the whole lattice, with free cells visibly around it and the world view showing the extent the garden will grow into — so the first impression is a place with space in it rather than a failure to have data.

With one card placed, assert RZ.extent() spans the full lattice — origin plus cols × pitch_x by rows × pitch_y — and not merely the bounding box of what is drawn, and that RZ.freeCell(2) returns a cell inside it. Zoom to fit: more of the board is empty than occupied.

Law 4 · C23

A new garden is loaded-and-empty, and it must be dressed as such. Day zero may never borrow the unknown material to look busier or softer: PlantPal has looked, and there is nothing, and that is a different sentence from “we have not fetched this”. Which materials those two are is settled by degradation; what this law forbids is using the wrong one here.

On the day-zero board assert no element carries data-state="unknown" and no vein carries data-unknown="true", while the bare cards carry data-state="empty". Assert each empty card states, in words, that it knows there is nothing.

Law 5 · C26, C9

Nothing stands on the board that the gardener does not own. No sample plants, no greyed-out demo fig, no placeholder species, and no padded count: a card on the plane is a claim that a record exists, and a zero is printed as zero. A first minute spent deleting a plant somebody never bought is worse than an honest bare shelf.

For every .rz-n on the day-zero board assert a real record id and that its printed recap count equals the length of the collection it stands for. Assert no card carries a sample, demo or placeholder flag, and that RZ.degree(id) equals the number printed anywhere that names it.

The day-zero board

Three cards, two veins, and a lot of room. My garden is the focus and it says nothing is here yet; Journal is loaded and bare; Care guides ships with the app and is already whole, so even on the first morning there is somewhere real to go. Travel to the guides and back — a one-plant world is small, not flat, and it hops the same way every world does.

Be honest about the shape of this: on day zero the graph is a couple of edges and does not yet look like the dense, cross-linked garden the rest of this system is drawn for. That is the one moment it is true, and pretending otherwise by drawing veins to things that do not exist would be the worse mistake.

Press Fit in the camera cluster and read the world view: the extent is the whole lattice, not the bounding box of three cards, so the map a gardener learns on their first morning is the map they keep. Looking around is not navigating — the camera changes neither where you are nor what you own.

RZ.mount({
  world: "#rz-world",
  plane: "#rz-plane",
  focus: "n-garden",
  path: ["n-garden"],
  edges: [
    ["n-garden", "n-guides"],
    ["n-garden", "n-journal"]
  ]
});

The first plant

Adding a plant changes data, so it is a stake — a filled form that never moves the camera. Ask for as little as PlantPal can start with: a name the gardener calls it by, and a photo if they have one. Everything else can be learned from the plant later, and the plant is theirs immediately either way.

The cell comes from the free-cell search, not from a judgement: RZ.freeCell(2) on the board above finds rows 0 and 2 taken and returns [2, 1]. The plant is written down at that cell and read back from it forever after. Say so to the gardener in the domain's own words — “it will stay on this shelf” — and never as a coordinate.

The card is the same contract as every other card in the system — the kind graduation on its head carries plant, its rank carries how far it is from wherever the gardener is standing, and both survive the text being unreadable. A first plant is not a special kind of node; it is the first instance of the ordinary one, owned by node.

What a theme fills

Day zero declares no token family of its own — that is the point of it. It is the ordinary node contract with two attributes set, and a theme dresses it by dressing those.

Attributes day zero sets
AttributeValueMeans
data-state empty Loaded, and known to hold nothing. Its material is --rz-empty, settled by degradation.
data-cell col,row Written once by the free-cell search at the moment of adding, and read back on every load. Never spoken to a gardener.
data-kind-key plant The first plant is an ordinary plant from its first second, so it carries the kind channel like any other.
data-recap “No plants yet” The domain's own words for zero. A count of nothing is still a count, and it is printed rather than hidden.

Day one — the world grows outward

The second card is where day zero pays off. The fig does not move. The species that arrives once a photo is identified takes its own free cell, a vein is drawn between them before anyone travels it, and the degree printed on the fig goes from zero to one because the degree genuinely changed. The gardener's second visit starts from a landmark they already have.

If the first identification is unsure, say so — this is the likeliest one to be wrong, and it is the first thing anyone learns about whether this app tells the truth. The plant is in the garden under the name they gave it either way; nothing waits on the model being certain. The instrument that grades it is vitals.

// the twelfth plant, and the thirteenth, take free cells —
// nothing already placed is renumbered or re-flowed:
RZ.freeCell(2);                  // -> [2, 1]  first plant
RZ.freeCell(2);                  // -> [2, 3]  next free row, same column
RZ.nodes["n-fig"].col;           // -> 2       unchanged, forever
RZ.degree("n-fig");              // -> 1       once the species is real

Accessibility

An empty world has to be announced as a place with something to do, not as an absence. Each card's accessible name is built by the runtime from its own kind, name, recap and real degree — “Collection · My garden · No plants yet · 2 veins out” — so a screen-reader user meets the same honest zero a sighted user reads, and never a cell, a row or a lattice coordinate. The world view's label is rewritten on every hop in lockstep with the visible you-are-here, and on day zero it says the same short truth.

When the first plant lands, announce it in the polite live region and leave focus where it is. The gardener is still in the form they just used; moving them into the world would be a traversal they did not ask for, and this system never travels without a user-attributable reason. Say what happened and where it went in the domain's words: “Office Fig is in your garden, on the shelf beside your journal.”

The one card that exists is reachable by keyboard from its first second, and the arrow keys will find its first neighbour the day it has one — a garden of one is still a graph, walked the same way. A direction with no vein is spoken rather than ignored, so silence never has to be interpreted. Colour carries nothing alone here: empty is a dashed rim, a different fill and a sentence. Logical properties throughout, so nothing points the wrong way in RTL. The full keyboard and announcement contract is accessibility.

Usage guidance

Every day-zero mistake is a flinch away from the truth: the world is bare, that feels thin, and the temptation is to dress it — with demo plants, with a tour, with a soft “not loaded yet” instead of an honest “nothing here”. Each of those costs the gardener the map they were about to learn.

Do

Draw the empty garden as a named place with a real count of zero and one stake worth pressing, laid out on the full lattice with the room around it visible (Law 3, Law 5, C26).

Don’t

Fill it with sample plants, even greyed out. A card on the plane claims a record exists, and a demo fig the gardener has to delete is a worse first minute than a bare shelf (C26, C9).

Do

Tell the gardener their plant is going somewhere it will stay, and mean it — the cell is written at the moment of adding and read back on every load (Law 2, C7).

Don’t

Leave the position to a layout that settles later. A garden that re-arranges itself between visits erases the only map the gardener had, and it breaks two loads' worth of determinism (C7, C8).

Do

Say plainly that PlantPal looked and found nothing, in the card that looked, and offer the one thing that would change it (Law 4, C23).

Don’t

Dress a new garden in the unknown material to soften it. Unknown means we have not fetched this; using it here tells the gardener something is missing when nothing is (C23).

Do

Let the first run be the world at its smallest scale, with anything worth saying said inside the card it applies to (Law 1, C4).

Don’t

Route them through a tour or a welcome flow first. It is a surface over the world that has to be left, and everything it teaches has to be re-learned in place anyway (C4, C2).

Two more, stated as rules. Never make the first plant wait on identification — it goes into the garden under the name the gardener gave it, and the species arrives beside it later, or not at all. And never move the camera on an add: putting a plant down is a mutation, the view stays where the gardener left it, and they travel to their new plant only if they ask to.
