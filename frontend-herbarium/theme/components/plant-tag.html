Plant tag

The plant tag is the stake pushed into the soil beside one plant: its nickname, its binomial, and the few facts that stay true between visits. It names a plant; it never reports what happened to it today.

Stable v1.0.0

Overview

Use a plant tag wherever one plant in my garden has to be identified at a glance — in the garden bed, at the head of a plant's own view, and as the result of a scan. It carries the plant's identity and its standing care facts: what it is, how often it drinks, how much light it wants.

Do not use a plant tag for anything that changes hour to hour. A watering that just happened belongs in the care log; a diagnosis belongs on a plate; a whole plant record with sections and actions belongs in a sheet. The tag stays still so the garden reads as a place rather than a feed.

One tag names exactly one plant. If you need to show ten plants, show ten tags in an .hbm-bed — never one tag summarising a group.

Specimen

Anatomy

The tag is drawn as a garden-centre stake: a punched hole at the top, rounded shoulders, a squared foot. Its four parts appear in this order and no other.

PartClassRule
Stake .hbm-tag The container. Draws the punched hole itself, so never add one. Width is capped at --hbm-measure-note — a tag never runs the full width of a view.
Nickname .hbm-tag__name The name the gardener gave the plant. Required, and always first — it is what they recognise.
Binomial .hbm-tag__binomial with .hbm-binomial Optional. Omit it when identification is unresolved rather than guessing one.
Facts .hbm-tag__facts / .hbm-tag__fact A description list of standing facts, two to four of them. Each dt is a one-word label, each dd a short value.

Nothing else is part of the tag. Buttons, health, confidence and dates are composed around it, never invented inside it.

Composition

A tag carries identity only, so anything that judges or acts is added as a sibling in the flow of the tag's body. These compositions are sanctioned; keep to them.

Tag with health

When the garden view has to answer "which of these needs attention", add a health reading under the facts. Health is a claim about the plant, not a fact printed on its stake, so it sits after the facts and keeps its own vocabulary.

Tag from a scan, when the name is a guess

A tag produced by identification must say how sure it is. Carry a .hbm-confidence reading always, and add a .hbm-hedge whenever confidence is low — the hedge is the herbarium's "cf.", the honest form of a guess. Never print an uncertain binomial as though it were settled.

Tag with an edge and an action

A tag is not itself clickable. Where the gardener can go to the plant's full record, draw that as an edge; where they can change the garden, draw that as a filled action. The two forms are told apart before either is used — see Edges and Actions.

Behaviour

The tag has no state of its own. It does not hover, select, expand or collapse, and it carries no interactive affordance — everything interactive inside it is a real control with its own states.

Changing values

When a fact changes — a watering interval, a due date, a health reading — the value updates in place. The tag does not move, reorder, reflow or reappear, so the gardener's eye stays where they left it. Never re-sort a bed of tags in response to a change the gardener just made.

Loading

A tag whose facts have not arrived keeps its stake and its nickname and marks only the unresolved values, using .hbm-growing on the individual dd. The geography does not move while things resolve.

<div class="hbm-tag__fact">
  <dt>Water</dt>
  <dd><span class="hbm-growing">Every 4 days</span></dd>
</div>

Failure

If a tag's facts fail to load, the failure lives inside the tag with its retry in place, as .hbm-node-error. Never replace the garden with a page-level error.

Reduced motion

The tag animates nothing, so prefers-reduced-motion changes nothing about it. Any control composed into it inherits the system's collapsed durations; do not add animation of your own.

Accessibility

Structure. Use <article> for the stake and a real heading for the nickname, at the level the surrounding page requires — h3 inside a section with an h2 above it. The facts are a description list, so a screen reader reads each label with its value as a pair.

Contrast. Every value in the tag comes from a semantic role, and the roles meet AA at the sizes they are used. The binomial is deliberately quiet but never below AA. Do not lower the opacity of a tag or any part of it.

Colour is never the only carrier. The health leaf and the confidence rungs each sit beside their words — "Needs attention", "Low confidence" — so a gardener who does not see the difference between watch and ailing still reads the state.

Keyboard. The tag is not focusable and takes no tab stop. Controls inside it are reached in document order: the action, then the edge. Focus is drawn by the system's own focus ring; never remove it.

Announcement. When a value changes in place, announce the change rather than the whole tag. Give a visually hidden live description of what changed:

<p class="hbm-visually-hidden" role="status">Basil watered. Next watering in 2 days.</p>

Binomial. The Latin name is supplementary. Never make it the only accessible name of a plant, and never require the gardener to read or type it.

Direction. The tag is built from logical properties and mirrors under RTL without changes. See RTL & locale.

Don't

Give the destination and the mutation the same form — the gardener cannot tell which one will change their garden.

Keep the stake short

Four facts is the ceiling. A tag that lists everything known about a plant has become a sheet, and the garden bed stops being scannable. Anything dated goes to the care log; anything uncertain follows Uncertainty.

The tag exists because a garden is a place you walk through, not a list you read. Its rules come from the six principles in PRINCIPLES.md.
