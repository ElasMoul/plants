Care log

The care log is one plant's field diary: every watering, feeding, repotting, scan and note, dated and kept in the order it happened. It is the record you scroll when you are trying to remember what you already tried.

Stable v1.0.0

Overview

Use the care log on a plant's own sheet, below its facts, to show what has happened to that one plant. Each entry is a thing that was done or observed, written as a short sentence in the past tense, with the date it happened. The log is history: it is appended to and it is never re-sorted, re-worded or quietly tidied.

Use it for one plant's history. Do not use it for what is coming next — anything due goes to Due today, which is a list of work, not a record. Do not use it as a feed of everything in my garden; a log belongs to the plant it describes, so the reader always knows whose history they are reading.

Where the entries come from

An entry is written by an action the person took, not by the app narrating itself. If nothing was done, nothing is logged — see the six principles.

Anatomy

A log is a list. Each entry carries a date, a body, and — when the entry records something the system inferred rather than something you did — the confidence that came with it. The ruled line down the side is the diary's margin; the last entry ends the rule, because nothing has happened after it yet.

Tokens this component reads

The log writes no value of its own. It takes its rule, its dot and its ink from semantic roles, so it re-inks itself in paper and dusk without a second definition.

TokenWhere it landsWhy
--hbm-border-rule The margin rule down each entry A ruled diary margin, visible but never louder than the writing.
--hbm-growth The dot at each entry Something grew here — the affirmative colour, not an alert colour.
--hbm-text-muted The date The date orients; the sentence is what you read.
--hbm-measure-note The body's width A note-length line, so a long history stays scannable.

Entry kinds

Every entry uses the same shape. What changes is what the sentence carries alongside it: a health reading, a confidence, a hedge, or nothing at all. Do not invent a new entry layout for a new kind of event.

In the product the log almost always lives in the body of a plant's sheet, under a plate label, with the action that writes the next entry in the sheet footer.

Behaviour

Writing an entry

A new entry is a mutation, so it is made with an .hbm-act — never with an edge. Wire the button with data-hbm-undo and the system offers the reversal on the button itself for the countdown you set. Nothing moves while the entry appears: the new entry is added at the top of the list, and the entries already there stay where they are.

<button type="button" class="hbm-act hbm-act--primary"
        data-hbm-undo="Watered Fern" data-hbm-undo-seconds="5">Log watering</button>

The full rules for actions, their tones and their reversals live on Actions.

Loading and staleness

A log that is still arriving keeps its geography: show the entry frames with .hbm-growing in place rather than a spinner where the list will be. A log you are reading from a cached copy says so with a stale note, so you never mistake an old record for a current one.

A log with nothing in it

A plant you added this morning has no history, and that is not a fault. Say what will fill the log rather than apologising for it. Use the empty state only when the log has genuinely loaded and is genuinely empty; a log that failed to load is an error in place, never a blank list.

Keyboard and motion

The log itself is text and takes no focus. Anything focusable inside it — the action that writes an entry, an edge to the scan behind a reading — is reached in reading order and shows the system focus ring. Under prefers-reduced-motion the durations collapse to almost nothing and the looping shimmer on .hbm-growing stops; the entries, their dates and their dots are still drawn, because they carry meaning rather than decoration. Do not add an animation to an entry as it arrives.

Accessibility

The log is a list, so mark it up as one: a ul with an li per entry gives a screen reader the count and the position without any ARIA at all. Do not replace it with stacked divs.

  • Naming. Give the log an accessible name that includes the plant, so a reader arriving from a list of plants knows whose history this is.
  • Dates. Wrap a relative date in a time element carrying the machine date in datetime, and add the exact date as visually hidden text where "yesterday" alone would be ambiguous read aloud.
  • Health and confidence. Both carry their meaning as words, not colour alone — "Needs attention", "Low". Never remove the word and leave the leaf or the rungs.
  • New entries. The entry appears with its own undo on the button that made it, and focus stays on that button, so a keyboard user is never moved somewhere they did not ask to go.
  • Contrast. Dates use the muted ink role and bodies the primary role; both clear the contrast floor in paper and dusk. Never write the date in a lighter value to calm the page down.

Usage guidance

Write each entry as one plain sentence about what happened. Say who did it, name the plant by its nickname, and stop. Guidance on wording lives in Voice & copy.

Do
  • Today · 08:10

    You watered Fern. Soil was dry to a fingertip.

Date every entry and say what was done in one past-tense sentence, so the log answers "when did I last do this?" at a glance.

Don't

  • Fern is overdue for water. Water it now.

Put an undated instruction in the log — a record of the past cannot also be a list of work, and the reader loses the one thing the log is for.

Do
Full history

Write entries with a filled action and reach the rest of the history along an edge, so the reader can tell what will change their garden before they touch it.

Don't

Draw the way into the history as another filled action — reading a record and writing to it then look identical.

Never rewrite history

An entry that was wrong is corrected by a new, dated entry saying so. Do not edit an old entry in place and do not delete one to tidy the list; the value of the log is that you can trust it.
