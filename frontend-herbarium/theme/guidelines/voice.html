Guidelines

Voice & copy

PlantPal speaks to a beginner gardener standing next to their plant with a phone in one hand. Every string is calm, specific, in the second person, and honest about what the system does not know.

Stable v1.0.0

Overview

This page governs every word the product ships: labels, hints, errors, empty states, toasts, and the sentences a plate or a diagnosis puts next to a photo. Use it whenever you write a string, and whenever you review one.

The register is paper, not console. Herbarium is drawn as a mounting sheet and a field notebook, and the copy matches: a careful person writing a short note about a living thing. Nothing shouts, nothing celebrates, nothing apologises at length. Read the six principles in PRINCIPLES.md before you write copy for a new surface — the words carry the same commitments the components do.

Do not use this page to decide what a component looks like or when a message appears. Placement is owned by the component pages — errors by Edges, uncertainty wording by Uncertainty, action labels by Actions. This page decides only how the sentence reads once its home is settled.

The five rules

Second person, present tense

Write to the person, not about the system. "Your Fern is due today", never "The system has determined that watering is required". The product never refers to itself in the third person and never says "we".

Specific over general

Name the plant, the date, and the act. "Watered Fern on 12 March" tells the gardener something; "Care event logged" does not. If a number is known, print it; if it is not known, say so rather than rounding it into confidence.

Rules as rules

State an instruction as an instruction. "Move Fern out of direct sun" — not "You might want to consider moving it". Hedged instructions read as uncertainty about the advice, which is different from uncertainty about the diagnosis, and the two must never blur.

Say when unsure

A low-confidence identification says so in words as well as in rungs. Plain doubt is a feature of this product: a gardener who is told "I am not sure" can go and look, while a gardener who is told the wrong name confidently will act on it.

No alarm

A struggling plant is a thing to attend to, not an emergency. No exclamation marks, no "Warning", no red-tinted verbs. Severity is carried by the component — a hbm-callout--harm, a hbm-health--ailing leaf — so the sentence itself stays level.

Vocabulary

PlantPal uses domestic words. A gardener says my garden, nickname, due today, needs attention, recent scans. Use those exact words; do not introduce a synonym for a concept that already has one.

SayNever sayWhy

due todayoverdue tasks, action itemsCare is a rhythm, not a backlog.
needs attentioncritical, at risk, failingA plant that is struggling can still be helped.
recent scanshistory, activity feed, query logThe gardener took photos; nothing was queried.
I'm not surelow confidence score, inconclusiveDoubt spoken plainly is act-on-able; a score is not.

Latin binomials belong in hbm-binomial and nowhere else. They are printed because a herbarium sheet prints them, never because the gardener has to read, know or type one. No string may require a binomial to make sense.

Writing doubt

When the system is unsure, three things are written at once: the rungs that show how unsure, the hedge that says so in words, and the one thing the gardener can do about it. Never ship the rungs alone — a drawn scale without a sentence leaves the reader to guess what "low" costs them.

The same discipline applies to a region that has not loaded. An unknown region says it is unknown; it never borrows the words of an empty one. Uncertainty owns when each of these appears.

Advice, caution and harm

Three callout tones carry three different stakes, and the sentence inside each must match the tone rather than repeat it. A harm callout does not need the word "warning"; the rule is the sentence.

Labels, hints and errors

A field is three strings and they divide the work cleanly: the label names the thing, the hint says what a good answer looks like, the error says what is wrong and what to type instead. Never move work between them — a hint that has to explain a rule is a rule that belongs in the label.

A failure inside a node is written the same way, and it keeps its retry in the sentence's reach. Never write a page-level apology.

Accessibility

Copy is the accessible layer of this product, so most of the accessibility work on this page is writing work.

  • Never write colour or shape into a sentence. "The red leaf" and "the item on the left" both fail — for colour vision, for screen readers, and under RTL. Name the plant and the state: "Fern needs attention".
  • Every drawn state needs a spoken equivalent. Confidence rungs, health leaves and status dots carry no text of their own; the words beside them are what a screen reader announces. If a state is only drawn, add a hbm-visually-hidden string.
  • Labels are announced, placeholders are not. Never move the label into the placeholder — the placeholder is an example of a good answer and disappears on the first keystroke.
  • Errors read after their field. Put the hbm-field__error string after the input and mark the input aria-invalid="true", so the field is announced as invalid and the sentence explaining why follows immediately.
  • Announce the act, not the mechanism. A toast says "Watered Fern", not "State updated" — the announcement is a report of what changed in the garden.
  • Write for translation. Full sentences, no strings assembled from fragments; a concatenated sentence cannot be reordered by a translator. See RTL & locale.

Usage guidance

Do
I'm not sure this is a Fern. A clearer photo would settle it.

Say the doubt in plain words and end on the one thing the gardener can do about it.

Don't
Identification returned a sub-threshold confidence score.

Report the machinery instead of the meaning — the reader learns nothing they can act on.

Do

Name the act in the button, so the label alone tells the gardener what will change in their garden.

Don't

Use a generic verb that names the form rather than the thing being done to the plant.

Do

Caution

Fern's leaves are browning at the tips. Move it away from the radiator.

Describe what was seen, then give one instruction — level tone, and the border carries the severity.

Don't

Caution

Urgent: your plant is dying! Act now to save it!

Raise alarm the component has already scaled — panic makes an ordinary care task unreadable.

Before you ship a string

Read it aloud once. If it sounds like a notification, a receipt or a status line rather than a person writing a short note about a plant, rewrite it.

  • It addresses the gardener as "you" and never calls the system "we".
  • It names the plant, the date or the act — not "the item".
  • It has no exclamation mark and no word of alarm.
  • It uses PlantPal's own vocabulary, and any binomial in it is decoration only.
  • It states doubt if there is doubt, in words as well as in rungs.
  • It is a whole sentence a translator can reorder.
